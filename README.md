🔍 What Is Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, networks, applications, and data from digital attacks, unauthorized access, and damage.

Its main goals are to:

Protect sensitive information
Keep systems and services running
Detect and respond to threats quickly
Reduce risk for individuals and organizations
🌍 Why Cybersecurity Matters
Personal, financial, and health data is stored online
Businesses depend on digital systems to operate
Critical infrastructure (power, water, hospitals) relies on networks
A single breach can cause financial loss, legal trouble, and loss of trust
🔺 The CIA Triad

The foundation of information security.

Principle	Meaning	Example
Confidentiality	Only authorized people can access data	Encryption, access controls
Integrity	Data stays accurate and unaltered	Hashing, digital signatures
Availability	Systems and data are accessible when needed	Backups, redundancy
📘 Key Terminology
Term	Meaning
Asset	Anything of value that needs protection (data, devices, people)
Vulnerability	A weakness that could be exploited
Threat	Anything that could cause harm to an asset
Exploit	A method that takes advantage of a vulnerability
Risk	The likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability
Attack Surface	All the points where a system can be attacked
Defense in Depth	Using multiple layers of security controls
🧩 Domains of Cybersecurity
Network Security: protecting networks with firewalls, IDS/IPS, and segmentation
Application Security: building and testing software securely
Information Security: protecting data in storage and in transit
Cloud Security: securing cloud infrastructure and services
Endpoint Security: protecting laptops, phones, and servers
Identity & Access Management (IAM): controlling who can access what
Operational Security (OpSec): processes for handling and protecting data
Incident Response: detecting, containing, and recovering from attacks
Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity: restoring operations after an incident
Security Awareness: training people to recognize risks
⚠️ Common Cyber Threats
Threat	Description
Phishing	Fake messages that trick users into revealing credentials or installing malware
Malware	Malicious software such as viruses, worms, trojans, and spyware
Ransomware	Encrypts files and demands payment for access
Social Engineering	Manipulating people instead of systems
DDoS	Overwhelming a service with traffic to make it unavailable
Man-in-the-Middle	Intercepting communication between two parties
SQL Injection / XSS	Web application flaws that let attackers manipulate data or scripts
Password Attacks	Brute force, credential stuffing, and password spraying
Zero-Day	Attacks on vulnerabilities not yet known to the vendor
Supply Chain Attacks	Compromising a trusted vendor or software dependency
🛠️ Core Security Controls

Controls are grouped by their purpose:

Type	Purpose	Examples
Preventive	Stop incidents before they happen	Firewalls, MFA, patching
Detective	Identify incidents as they happen	Logging, IDS, SIEM, monitoring
Corrective	Fix damage after an incident	Backups restore, malware removal
Deterrent	Discourage bad behavior	Warning banners, policies
Compensating	Alternative control when the primary one is not possible	Extra monitoring on a legacy system

They can also be technical (software, hardware), administrative (policies, training), or physical (locks, cameras).

🔑 Authentication & Access Control

Authentication proves who you are. Authorization decides what you can do. Accounting records what you did (together: AAA).

Authentication factors:

Something you know: password, PIN
Something you have: phone, security key, smart card
Something you are: fingerprint, face

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) combines two or more factors and greatly reduces account takeover risk.

Access control principles:

Least privilege: give only the access needed
Separation of duties: no single person controls a whole critical process
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC): permissions assigned by job role
Zero Trust: never trust, always verify
🔒 Cryptography Basics
Concept	Description
Symmetric encryption	One shared key encrypts and decrypts (e.g., AES)
Asymmetric encryption	Public key encrypts, private key decrypts (e.g., RSA)
Hashing	One-way fingerprint of data used to verify integrity (e.g., SHA-256)
Digital signatures	Prove who created data and that it was not changed
TLS/HTTPS	Encrypts traffic between browsers and websites
Certificates (PKI)	Bind public keys to verified identities
🌐 Network Security Basics
Firewall: filters traffic based on rules
IDS / IPS: detects (and can block) suspicious activity
VPN: encrypted tunnel over a public network
Network Segmentation: separating networks to limit damage
DMZ: isolated zone for public-facing services
Wi-Fi Security: use WPA3 (or WPA2) with a strong passphrase
🚨 Incident Response Lifecycle

Based on the NIST model:

Preparation: plans, tools, and training
Detection & Analysis: identify and confirm the incident
Containment, Eradication & Recovery: stop the spread, remove the cause, restore systems
Post-Incident Activity: review lessons learned and improve

✅ Best Practices Checklist
 Use strong, unique passwords and a password manager
 Enable MFA on all important accounts
 Keep operating systems and apps updated
 Back up data regularly (3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, 2 media types, 1 offsite)
 Encrypt sensitive data at rest and in transit
 Use antivirus/EDR and a firewall
 Apply least privilege to all accounts
 Be cautious with links, attachments, and unknown senders
 Avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive tasks, or use a VPN
 Have and practice an incident response plan
